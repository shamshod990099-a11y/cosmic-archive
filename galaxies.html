01. What Is a Galaxy?

A galaxy is a vast gravitationally bound system containing stars, stellar remnants, gas, dust and dark matter.

Galaxies can contain enormous numbers of stars and can vary greatly in size, mass, structure and history.

They are not isolated islands in space. Galaxies interact with one another and exist within larger structures such as galaxy groups, clusters and the cosmic web.

Key idea: A galaxy is a complex ecosystem of matter, gravity, radiation, star formation and cosmic evolution.

02. Our Milky Way Galaxy

Earth is located inside the Milky Way, a large barred spiral galaxy.

Our Solar System lies in one of the galaxy's spiral-arm regions, far from the galactic center.

The Milky Way contains hundreds of billions of stars according to commonly used astronomical estimates, although the exact number is not known.

At its center is the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A*.

OUR COSMIC ADDRESS

Earth → Solar System → Milky Way → Local Group → larger cosmic structures.

04. Spiral Galaxies

Spiral galaxies have a flattened rotating disk and generally contain a central bulge.

Many also contain prominent spiral arms.

Spiral arms are not necessarily rigid structures made from the same stars. They can behave more like large-scale patterns moving through the galactic disk.

Gas, dust and regions of active star formation are often concentrated along these structures.

🌌

Disk

A flattened rotating region containing stars, gas and dust.

✨

Spiral Arms

Large-scale structures where dense regions can trigger star formation.

🟠

Bulge

A dense central concentration of stars.

05. Elliptical Galaxies

Elliptical galaxies range from nearly spherical systems to highly elongated shapes.

They generally contain less cold gas and dust than actively star-forming spiral galaxies.

Their stellar populations are often dominated by older stars, although individual galaxies can have more complicated histories.

Massive elliptical galaxies can contain enormous numbers of stars and can reside near the centers of galaxy clusters.

06. Irregular Galaxies

Irregular galaxies do not have the clear spiral or elliptical structure seen in the major regular galaxy classes.

Their shapes can be influenced by gravitational interactions, mergers and internal processes.

Some irregular galaxies contain large amounts of gas and can experience intense episodes of star formation.

07. Main Components of a Galaxy

A galaxy is made from several interacting components.

⭐

Stars

Stars contain most of the visible mass of many galaxies and produce much of their emitted light.

☁️

Gas

Gas provides the raw material from which new stars can form.

🌫️

Dust

Dust absorbs and scatters light and plays an important role in star-forming environments.

🌑

Dark Matter

An unseen component inferred from its gravitational effects on visible matter and light.

08. Stars Inside Galaxies

Galaxies contain stars of many different ages, masses and chemical compositions.

Young massive stars can illuminate surrounding gas and are important indicators of recent star formation.

Older stars provide information about the galaxy's earlier history.

By studying stellar populations, astronomers can reconstruct parts of a galaxy's formation and evolution.

09. Gas and Dust

Gas is essential for galaxy evolution because stars form from dense clouds of gas.

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in ordinary cosmic gas.

Dust consists of tiny solid particles made from heavier elements and compounds.

Dust can hide regions of star formation at visible wavelengths, which is why astronomers also observe galaxies using infrared and radio telescopes.

10. Dark Matter

Dark matter is a form of matter that does not appear to interact strongly with electromagnetic radiation.

Its presence is inferred primarily through gravity.

The motions of stars and gas in galaxies provide some of the strongest evidence that galaxies contain much more mass than can be accounted for by visible matter alone.

Important: The exact physical nature of dark matter remains unknown. Its gravitational effects are well studied, but the particle or particles responsible have not yet been identified.

11. Galaxy Rotation

Galaxies rotate, and their rotation provides important information about their mass distribution.

Astronomers can measure how quickly stars and gas move around a galactic center.

The observed rotation of many galaxies cannot be explained by visible matter alone under standard gravity models.

This is one of the major observational foundations for the dark-matter hypothesis.

v² / r = GM / r² Simplified circular-orbit relation

12. How Galaxies Form

Galaxy formation began from tiny density variations in the early universe.

Gravity amplified these differences over cosmic time.

Dark matter formed large gravitational structures, while ordinary matter fell into these potential wells, cooled and eventually formed stars and galaxies.

The process was not a single event. Galaxies have continued to grow and change throughout cosmic history.

13. Galaxy Evolution

Galaxies evolve through a combination of internal and external processes.

Star formation, stellar explosions, gas cooling, black-hole activity and gravitational interactions can all influence a galaxy's evolution.

Astronomers study galaxies at different distances because looking farther away also means looking further back in time.

COSMIC TIME MACHINE

Because light travels at a finite speed, distant galaxies are observed as they appeared in the past.

14. Galaxy Mergers

Galaxies can interact gravitationally and sometimes merge.

During a merger, gravitational forces can distort stellar distributions and compress gas.

These interactions can trigger bursts of star formation.

The final galaxy can have a structure very different from either original galaxy.

Galaxies are not static objects. They are constantly shaped by gravity.

15. Galaxy Clusters

Galaxies can be gravitationally bound into groups and clusters.

A galaxy cluster can contain hundreds or even thousands of galaxies together with enormous amounts of hot gas and dark matter.

The hot intracluster gas can reach temperatures of millions of degrees and emit X-rays.

Gravitational lensing can also reveal the mass distribution within clusters.

16. The Cosmic Web

On the largest scales, galaxies are not distributed randomly.

They form an enormous network of filaments, walls, clusters and relatively empty regions called voids.

This structure is known as the cosmic web.

Dark matter plays a major role in creating the gravitational framework of this large-scale structure.

🕸️

Filaments

🌌

Clusters

⚫

Voids

Vast regions containing relatively few galaxies.

17. Black Holes in Galaxies

Many large galaxies contain supermassive black holes near their centers.

The Milky Way contains Sagittarius A*, while the center of the galaxy M87 contains M87*.

When a supermassive black hole accretes large amounts of gas, the surrounding region can become extraordinarily luminous.

These active galactic nuclei can influence their host galaxies through radiation, winds and jets.

18. Quasars

A quasar is an extremely luminous active galactic nucleus powered by accretion onto a supermassive black hole.

The accretion process can convert gravitational energy into enormous amounts of electromagnetic radiation.

Some quasars are so bright that they can be observed across vast cosmological distances.

Quasars are not stars. Their enormous brightness comes from processes occurring around an actively feeding supermassive black hole.

19. How Astronomers Measure Galaxy Distances

Measuring astronomical distances is one of the fundamental challenges in astronomy.

Astronomers use a sequence of methods often described as the cosmic distance ladder.

Nearby objects can be measured using geometric techniques such as parallax.

More distant galaxies can be studied using objects with known relationships between brightness and intrinsic properties, along with cosmological measurements.

20. Cosmological Redshift

Light from distant galaxies is often shifted toward longer wavelengths.

This is called redshift.

In cosmology, redshift is closely connected with the expansion of space between the source and the observer.

z = (λobserved − λemitted) / λemitted Definition of redshift

Measuring redshift helps astronomers determine how galaxies participate in the expanding universe.

22. Summary

Galaxies are enormous gravitational systems containing stars, gas, dust, dark matter and often supermassive black holes.

They come in different forms, including spiral, elliptical, lenticular and irregular galaxies.

Galaxies form and evolve through gravity, star formation, gas dynamics, mergers and interactions with their environment.

On the largest scales, galaxies form part of the cosmic web — an enormous structure extending across the observable universe.
